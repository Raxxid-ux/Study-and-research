Module 01

1. Brain Basics, Neurons, and How the Brain Develops

To understand how we make choices, we first have to understand the physical machine running the show. The brain is built from billions of interconnected cells that communicate using electrical pulses and chemical messengers. It does not arrive fully built at birth; it spends over two decades wiring, trimming, and fine-tuning its connections.

The Three Main Evolutionary Parts

1. Brainstem and Cerebellum

The oldest, most primitive parts of the brain. They handle automatic survival functions like your heartbeat, breathing, balance, and basic reflexes.

2. Limbic System

Buried under the cortex, this network manages emotions, stress responses, and survival drives like feeding and reproduction. Its two heavy hitters are the hippocampus (vital for forming new memories, spatial navigation, and adult neurogenesis) and the amygdala (an almond-shaped structure that tags events with emotion and fear).

3. Cerebral Cortex

The wrinkled outer "grey matter" layer made of six distinct cellular sheets, divided into two hemispheres by the longitudinal fissure and joined in the middle by the corpus callosum. Its bumps are called gyri and its grooves are called sulci.

The Four Cortical Lobes

Frontal Lobe Separated by central sulcus

Located at the front, separated from the parietal lobe by the central sulcus. It is the seat of personality and executive functions: planning ahead, impulse control, social rules, and decision-making.

Everyday Example: The Two-Point Clip Test

If you unbend a paper clip so it has two points a few millimeters apart, you can easily tell there are two distinct points touching your sensitive fingertip or tongue. But if you press those same two points against your upper arm or back, your parietal cortex only feels a single dull press because sensory receptors there are spaced much farther apart.

Brain Cells (Neurons vs. Glia)

Neurons (~85-100 billion):

The primary information processors. They consist of a soma (cell body holding the nucleus), branching dendrites (which receive incoming messages), and a long axon (which carries electrical impulses away toward other cells).

• Sensory neurons: bring environmental inputs inward.

• Motor neurons: send movement commands to muscles.

• Pyramidal neurons: pyramid-shaped excitatory cells heavily involved in advanced thinking and decision-making.

Glial Cells:

The non-electrical support staff that outnumber neurons.

• Schwann cells: wrap axons in a fatty myelin sheath (leaving tiny gaps called nodes of Ranvier) to make electrical signals leap faster.

• Astrocytes: maintain chemical balance and support new cell growth.

• Microglia: act as waste-scavengers and fight inflammation.

Everyday Example: Phineas Gage (1848)

In 1848, a blasting explosion shot an iron rod through railroad worker Phineas Gage's frontal lobe. He survived, walked, and talked normally, but his personality shifted dramatically: he went from being reliable and polite to impulsive, rude, and incapable of sticking to plans. This historic case proved the frontal lobe controls our personality and social decisions.

Brain Development Timeline: Over Two Decades of Wiring

1. PRENATAL

Starts 5 weeks after conception; neurons multiply rapidly up to 100 billion, migrate to assigned zones, and begin forming synapses and early myelin.

2. NEWBORN

Brain weighs 350-400g. Synapses are strongest in survival and sensory areas (vision and hearing myelinate quickly).

3. CHILDHOOD

Hits ~90% adult volume by age 7. Rapid synaptogenesis (building connections) followed by synaptic pruning (chopping unused links).

4. ADOLESCENCE

Total volume peaks at 11-14. Dopamine inputs to frontal areas surge; aggressive pruning and myelination streamline circuits.

5. ADULTHOOD

Prefrontal cortex finishes maturing around age 25. Plasticity never stops; brains reshape connections throughout entire lives.

Topographic Organization and How We Study Networks:
Topographic mapping: Neighboring points in the body or environment connect to neighboring points in the brain, keeping spatial layouts organized and saving wiring energy.
Anatomical tract tracing: Injects fluorescent tracers into animal brains to track physical pathways under a microscope with high precision.
ERP/EEG: Records electrical waves on the human scalp during tasks to time-lock specific choices down to the millisecond.
Module 1 Key Takeaways
  • Your brain builds most of its 100 billion neurons before birth, but cognitive development is mostly about pruning and insulating (myelinating) those connections later on.
  • Communication inside a neuron is electrical (action potential), but communication between neurons is chemical (neurotransmitters across the synapse).
  • The cerebral cortex is mapped topographically, meaning physical relationships in the real world are preserved as spatial maps in the brain.
  • The prefrontal cortex matures last, taking until about age 25 to finalize its wiring.
Module 02

2. The Prefrontal Cortex: The Brain's CEO and Decision Engine

The prefrontal cortex (PFC) sits at the very front of the frontal lobe and serves as the brain's executive control center. While older sensory areas just register what is happening right now, your PFC lets you hold goals in mind, imagine things that do not exist yet, weigh future consequences, and choose your actions deliberately.

Anatomical Definition: Defined as the region receiving inputs from the mediodorsal nucleus of the thalamus.
Massive Expansion: Makes up about 29% of the entire human cortex (evolutionarily newest in mammals).
25-Year Maturation: Higher-order strategic planning and impulse control develop much later than basic memory or movement.

Subregions and Their Jobs

Medial PFC

Focuses on motivation, drive, and focused attention.

Orbital PFC (Orbitofrontal)

Acts as your impulse control center, keeping risky or inappropriate behaviors in check.

Lateral PFC

Manages working memory, goal-directed planning, and complex multi-step strategies.

Cross-Species Comparisons (The Rat Model):

Humans and primates have a specialized "granular" prefrontal cortex that rats physically lack. However, the medial prefrontal cortex of rats shows functional and anatomical homology to primate prefrontal regions. This means studying medial PFC circuits in rats gives us reliable clues about human issues like addiction, depression, and psychosis.

Anterior Pole: Abstract Evaluating Highest Hierarchy

Research by Dr. Stacey Bedwell shows that as you move toward the anterior (front pole) of the PFC, wiring becomes non-aligned, overlapping, and far more complex. This matches cognitive findings: the anterior pole sits at the very top of the hierarchy, handling the most abstract, open-ended evaluations.

Everyday Example: Filtering Frustration at Work

If your boss gives an unfair instruction, your immediate emotional impulse might be to shout. An intact PFC applies top-down inhibition, weighing the consequences of getting fired against the satisfaction of venting, allowing you to stay calm and respond professionally.

Core Theories of PFC Function

Integrative Association Hub:

The PFC does not perform one single job; it gathers sensory data, emotions, and past memories from across the whole brain to construct an abstract mental workspace.

Dynamic Filtering Theory:

The PFC acts as a filter, dampening irrelevant distractions and emotional noise so you can focus on the goal at hand.

Miller and Cohen's Integrative Theory:

The PFC builds top-down rules and goals, sending signals back out to other brain regions to steer your thoughts and physical actions.

Four Everyday Types of Decisions

1. Routine Choices

Everyday low-stakes decisions where you have little control over the options (e.g., picking a brand of milk off a grocery shelf).

2. Making Outcomes

Decisions where your personal effort and self-efficacy directly determine success (e.g., planning a project schedule).

3. Risky Decisions

Choices with uncertain, unpredictable outcomes. Adolescents make significantly more risky choices because their PFC wiring is still undergoing heavy pruning and myelination.

4. Strategic Decisions

High-stakes life choices (e.g., changing jobs, buying a house) that require pulling up past knowledge to calculate long-term costs and rewards.

Module 2 Key Takeaways
  • The human PFC takes up nearly a third of the cortex and takes roughly 25 years to fully wire up.
  • The PFC acts as an association engine, integrating sensory inputs, past memories, and emotional states to run top-down rules.
  • Prefrontal anatomy changes across an anterior-to-posterior gradient, with the front-most areas handling abstract reasoning.
  • Teenagers naturally lean into risky decision-making because their prefrontal inhibitory circuits are still developing.
Module 03

3. Neurotransmitters, Stress, and Decision Networks

Decisions are not made in a vacuum; they are powered by brain-wide chemical systems and modulated by stress. While amino acid transmitters act like rapid on/off switches, neuromodulator systems project broadly across the cortex to adjust mood, attention, arousal, and value.

Amino Acids (Local Switches):

The primary local on/off switches. Excitatory transmitters act like an accelerator pedal, while inhibitory transmitters act like a brake to keep brain activity balanced.

Neurotransmitter Systems (Broad Modulators):

Produced by small groups of cells subcortically (brainstem and midbrain) whose long axons spray outward across the entire cortex to modulate brain states.

The Three Big Modulators in Decision-Making

Serotonin (5-HT) Raphe Nuclei Origin

Originates in brainstem clusters (like the raphe nuclei in the midbrain, pons, and medulla) and projects to the limbic system, thalamus, and cortex. It regulates mood, sleep, appetite, and the creation of new memories. Low serotonin impairs new memory formation and is tied to depression; SSRIs treat depression by blocking its reuptake back into the presynaptic neuron, leaving more serotonin in the cleft.

Noradrenaline (Norepinephrine) Locus Coeruleus Origin

Produced in the locus coeruleus and projects straight to the thalamus, hypothalamus, and PFC. It drives arousal, vigilance, and focused attention. Noradrenergic neurons shut off during REM sleep; disruptions in this system are heavily linked to attention deficits in ADHD.

Dopamine (DA) 3 Major Pathways

Has three major pathways (nigrostriatal, mesolimbic, and mesocortical). It is the chemical engine of motivation, reward anticipation, novelty seeking, and working memory. Antipsychotics often work as D2-receptor blockers (antagonists) to quiet down overactive dopamine transmission.

The HPA Axis and the Stress Response

The physiological feedback loop governing emergency decision modes.

Hypothalamus Pituitary Adrenals

When the amygdala detects a threat, it signals the hypothalamus, activating the HPA axis to release cortisol into the bloodstream.

Cortisol floods the body with glucose for a fight-or-flight response and teams up with adrenaline to forge vivid, high-priority memories (flashbulb memories) so you remember how to avoid that danger later.

Everyday Example: Flashbulb Stress Memories

If you are attacked by an aggressive dog on a specific jogging trail, your HPA axis floods your brain with cortisol. That surge burns the sensory details of the trail into your memory, ensuring that the next time you approach that corner, your rational and experiential systems steer you down a different path.

Cognitive Decision Models

Epstein's Cognitive-Experiential Self-Theory:

We have two parallel decision engines. The rational system is slow, analytical, conscious, effortful, and relies on the PFC and hippocampus. The experiential system is fast, automatic, emotional, intuitive, and driven by the amygdala and striatum.

Shallice's Supervisory System:

Everyday routine tasks run on contention scheduling (unconscious, learned action scripts called schemas). When you encounter a completely new or conflicting problem, the Supervisory Attentional System (SAS) intervenes to deliberately steer behavior.

Stuss & Benson's Hierarchy:

A three-tier pyramid: automatic sensory routines at the bottom, goal-directed executive control in the middle, and metacognition (self-reflection on your own thought processes) at the very top.

The Red Traffic Light (Contention vs SAS):

When a red light flashes, your brain executes a practiced schema via contention scheduling—foot off gas, onto brake. But if an ambulance speeds the wrong way through the intersection, your SAS steps in instantly to override the green light and brake.

Module 3 Key Takeaways
  • Local amino acids provide fast on/off signaling, while subcortical systems (DA, 5-HT, NA) broadcast mood and reward states brain-wide.
  • Epstein's model splits thinking into a fast "experiential" system and a deliberate "rational" system.
  • Frontal damage leaves automatic schemas intact (contention scheduling works) but breaks ability to adapt to brand-new situations (SAS fails).
  • Decision control runs from simple physical actions at the back to abstract context and self-reflection at the front.
Module 04

4. Decision-Making Styles and Childhood Influences

People approach choices using characteristic habits known as decision-making styles. These styles are not hardcoded at birth; they are shaped throughout childhood by attachment, environmental stability, and social interactions.

Defining Decision-Making Style: A habitual, learned pattern a person uses when gathering information, weighing options, and making choices.

Early Theoretical Milestones

McKinney & Keene (1974):

Split thinkers into systematic (structured, logical step-followers) versus intuitive (exploratory, trial-and-error learners).

Driver (1979):

Proposed that styles are learned behaviors based on how much data someone collects and how many alternatives they review, suggesting people have primary and secondary styles.

Harren (1979):

Established three core approaches: Rational (takes personal ownership, plans logically), Intuitive (takes personal ownership, follows internal gut feelings), and Dependent (defers ownership onto other people).

Scott & Bruce's Five GDMS Styles

General Decision Making Style
1. Rational

Methodical, slow, gathers all facts, and systematically weighs alternatives.

2. Intuitive

Relies heavily on gut hunches, feelings, and what "feels right" in the moment.

3. Dependent

Uncomfortable deciding alone; constantly seeks advice, validation, and direction from others.

4. Avoidant

Feels anxious about choices; procrastinates and only decides when forced by deadlines.

5. Spontaneous

Impulsive and fast; makes snap choices just to get through the process without overthinking.

Everyday Example: Buying a Car Across the Five Styles
  • • Rational: Builds a spreadsheet comparing fuel economy and safety ratings.
  • • Intuitive: Sits in the driver's seat and buys it because it "feels right".
  • • Dependent: Brings three family members along to choose for them.
  • • Avoidant: Leaves the broken car in the driveway for months rather than face the dealership.
  • • Spontaneous: Signs the paperwork on the first car parked by the door just to get it over with.

How Childhood Trauma & Sibling Conflict Alter the Machinery

Childhood Trauma Dose-Response:

The PFC has an extended developmental window, making its developing synapses vulnerable to severe stressors. Repeated childhood trauma creates a "dose-response" effect: frequent trauma chronically dysregulates the HPA axis, flooding the system with cortisol and reducing synaptic plasticity. Early trauma impairs executive functions and working memory, often pushing adults toward extreme avoidant or purely intuitive styles.

Dr. Bedwell's Lab: Sibling Aggression:

Sibling conflict is common, but using aggression to establish dominance and hoard possessions alters prefrontal development. Counterintuitively, it is the perpetrators who used aggression to maintain dominance who are significantly more likely to display an avoidant decision-making style as adults, driven by underlying social anxiety and fear of failure.

The Crucial Task Distinction:

While general trauma increases real-time reckless behavior in everyday life, sibling aggression does not predict risky decision-making on laboratory tasks, showing that everyday family conflict affects decision styles differently than major trauma.

Module 4 Key Takeaways
  • Decision styles are learned behavioral habits, and most people blend styles depending on the situation.
  • Chronic avoidance of decisions is strongly linked to higher anxiety and worse mental health outcomes (Bavol'ar & Orosova, 2015).
  • Severe early trauma alters the physical development of the HPA axis and prefrontal cortex, locking people into rigid cognitive patterns.
  • Childhood sibling aggression specifically predicts adult avoidance (in those driven by dominance), but it does not drive risky gambling choices.
Module 05

5. Decision Deficits and Clinical Conditions

A decision-making deficit occurs when brain circuits are disrupted, leaving an individual unable to adapt to changing rules, weigh rewards against punishments, or resist impulsive actions. Comparing these breakdowns across clinical conditions shows how fragile the prefrontal network really is.

Substance Use (Stimulants) Rigid Habits

Chronic cocaine or methamphetamine abuse dumps massive amounts of dopamine into the striatum and prefrontal circuits. This accelerates the transition from flexible, goal-directed behavior into rigid, automated habits.

Reversal Learning Deficit: Patients become "stuck"; even when rules change and a behavior causes immediate harm, their brains fail to update, keeping them locked in compulsive drug-seeking.
Schizophrenia Connectivity

Characterized by positive symptoms (delusions, hallucinations) and negative symptoms (blunted affect, withdrawal). Structural scans show reduced prefrontal white matter volume, altered cellular organization, and smaller frontal lobes.

Jumping to Conclusions: Excessive dopamine in the striatum impairs how the brain integrates cortical information, causing patients to "jump to conclusions" based on very little data and persist with bad choices.
Autism Spectrum Win-Stay-Lose-Shift

Adults with autism report that decision-making is mentally exhausting, overwhelming, and anxiety-provoking, often leading to decision avoidance. Quick decisions or sudden disruptions to routines are especially difficult.

Feedback Rigidity: On experimental tasks, they often rely heavily on a rigid win-stay-lose-shift strategy, working harder for rewards while showing reduced internal sensitivity to reward feedback.
Major Depression Frontostriatal

Characterized by blunted striatal and prefrontal responses to positive rewards, along with low serotonin transmission in the PFC. Anhedonia (inability to feel pleasure) is directly tied to frontostriatal reward circuit breakdown. Depleted serotonin leads to cognitive rigidity.

Depressive Realism: Mild depression sometimes evaluates lack of control more accurately than non-depressed people.

Analytical Rumination: May have evolved to analyze complex social dilemmas.

Psychopathy / APD Amygdala-PFC

Marked by shallow emotional affect, callousness, impulsivity, and grandiosity. Driven by a functional disconnect between the amygdala (fear and punishment) and the prefrontal cortex (anticipating future consequences).

Zero Warning Signal: Because they do not feel the anticipatory emotional warning signal of punishment, they make reckless choices focused purely on immediate gratification.
Rehabilitation (CRT) Neuroplasticity

While medication rarely restores damaged decision-making logic on its own, behavioral therapies like Cognitive Remediation Therapy (CRT) train the brain through targeted mental exercises to adopt alternative problem-solving strategies.

Broken Traffic Light Example: If rules flip so green=stop and red=go, neurotypicals adapt quickly. Stimulant or schizophrenia patients keep driving through green and crashing due to frontostriatal inflexibility.

High-Yield Synthesis

Quick Revision Sheet

Development Lag:

The brain reaches full physical size in early adolescence (11-14), but the prefrontal cortex continues pruning and myelinating until roughly age 25.

Integrated Control:

The prefrontal cortex acts as an association hub. It does not work alone; it integrates emotional inputs from the amygdala and memory from the hippocampus to direct thoughts and behavior.

Dual Engines:

Decisions balance fast, emotional, automatic systems (experiential / contention scheduling) with slow, deliberate, top-down systems (rational / SAS).

Experiences Build Biology:

Childhood environments physically alter development. Chronic stress dysregulates the HPA axis, while early dominance aggression with siblings alters prefrontal development, leading to an avoidant decision style in adulthood.

Most Critical Distinctions

Action Potential vs. Synaptic Transmission:

Electrical signal running inside one cell vs. chemical molecules floating between two cells.

Synaptogenesis vs. Pruning:

Building millions of new connections vs. cutting away the weak, unused ones to increase speed and efficiency.

Routine Choices vs. Making Outcomes:

Picking fixed options where you have zero control (buying milk) vs. situations where your confidence and effort change the outcome (running a project).

Sibling Aggression vs. General Trauma:

Severe trauma triggers reckless, impulsive gambling; sibling dominance aggression specifically builds adult avoidance habits without increasing real-time gambling risk.
